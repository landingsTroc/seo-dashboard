The single biggest finding

One page ranks first for 3,700 searches a month and receives about 15 visits

/what-is-a-brand-ambassador/ holds position 1 for “brand ambassador meaning”. Normal click-through for that position would be roughly 1,000 visits a month. The gap is around 1,021 visits, and nothing else on the site comes close — the second-worst case is 45.

The cause is not a broken title. Ahrefs reports the search results for that query carry an AI Overview, image and video thumbnails, sitelinks and People Also Ask. Google answers the question itself. All six keywords in that cluster behave the same way.

So the win there is not the click, it is being cited inside the AI answer. The page already opened with a direct 30-word definition, which is right, but carried no FAQ, no schema and no comparison table. It now has seven questions with FAQPage markup, all answered from its own content. Chasing this with title tweaks would have been wasted effort.

What shipped

Every change was written through the API with the previous value backed up per record and the saved result read back and compared.

FAQ schema across 57 pages

The full brief — all three rounds — is complete: 57 URLs, every one carrying exactly one valid FAQPage, 306 questions in structured data, zero invalid blocks. Verified by parsing the live HTML of each page, not by searching for the tag.

A batch of 22 files was supplied. Auditing the pages first split them three ways, and only half needed the supplied content:

Pasting the supplied files onto the eight that already had questions would have produced two contradicting FAQ sections on one page, and schema that disagrees with visible content breaches Google’s guidelines.

The supplied questions needed rewriting

They were written as comprehension questions about each article — “What is the article’s main claim about how AI affects physical retail?”, “How does the article define B malls?” — with 62 self-referential phrases across 16 of the 22 files. Those validate as schema but nobody types them into Google and no AI assistant will cite them, which is the opposite of the brief’s stated purpose. All 55 were rewritten into search form, keeping every fact from the source files.

What the reports actually showed

Four audit exports described a larger problem than exists, or the wrong problem entirely.

The “site is down” report was a crawl against an empty cache

About 200 URLs returned 504. Ten minutes later the same URLs returned 10 of 10 at 200. Cold renders take 8 to 25 seconds; warm ones take under three. The crawl ran two minutes before a cache purge from this side, so it met a site rebuilding every page.

The real defect underneath is genuine and pre-dates this work: the site depends entirely on its cache never emptying. The 94/100 Core Web Vitals score measures cached delivery, so it hides this completely.

“94 orphan pages” is really three

Building the site’s link graph showed that /site-index/ alone is the only inbound link for 88 of the 94, and it returned 504 during that same crawl. One slow page failing explains the entire report. Only three pages genuinely have no links at all.

“83 pages where the title does not match” is 16

Google rewrites titles routinely; that is not a defect. Sixty-seven of the 83 were Google adding or trimming “- T-ROC”. Three titles were genuinely weaker than Google’s rewrite and were fixed, including the highest-traffic page in that report, whose title had lost its subject entirely.

The Hindi search result is not a fault

One page appears in Google with a Devanagari title. The page serves zero Hindi characters and declares English. It is Google’s own translation for Hindi queries from India. The eleven language-prefixed URLs all canonicalise correctly to the English original.

Blocked, and on whom

None of this can move from the WordPress side.

One WP Engine setting clears 436 audit errors

Every address with www does this: https://www.trocglobal.com/x/ redirects to http://trocglobal.com/x/, which then redirects back to HTTPS. Two hops, one of them insecure, on every URL.

That is why “HTTPS to HTTP redirect: 218” and “Redirect chain: 218” report the same number — they are the same 218 URLs. All 267 redirect rules were checked and none points at HTTP, so this sits at platform level. The ask is simply that the www redirect keeps the HTTPS scheme.
